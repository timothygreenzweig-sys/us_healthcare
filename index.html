How prevalence shifts with age

Most conditions climb steeply with age — but not all the same way. Blood pressure and diabetes rise relentlessly; obesity peaks in middle age and eases in the oldest group. Bands differ slightly by survey; each is labeled.

Real-time surveillance

The chronic-condition data in the other views comes from surveys refreshed every 1–2 years — there is no live feed for it. The one part of U.S. population health that is tracked in near-real-time is infectious respiratory disease, via emergency-department visits and wastewater. Why this isn't auto-updating

  • This page is a self-contained document and can't call live data services, so these values are a snapshot, not a live stream. Re-open the CDC dashboards below for the current week, or ask me to refresh the page.
  • CDC updates respiratory and wastewater data every Friday. Wastewater often gives the earliest signal because it doesn't depend on people seeking care.

How to read this — and what it doesn't say

  • The figures overlap. One person often lives with several conditions at once, so prevalences can't be summed.
  • It's a patchwork of surveys. Overall figures use the most authoritative source per condition (often NHANES, which physically measures people); the age, trend, and state views use BRFSS (self-reported by phone), which runs lower. Compare within a view, not across.
  • Many cases are undiagnosed — especially prediabetes, diabetes, kidney disease, and high blood pressure. Self-reported views miss people who don't know they're affected (e.g., BRFSS kidney disease ~4% vs. ~14% when measured).
  • Prevalence isn't severity, and estimates carry a margin of error.
